\title{Accuracy, Failure Modes and Measured Runtime of\\
Visual-Inertial Odometry and SLAM Systems\\
under a Common Containerized Protocol}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Terrence Alvianto Sutardi}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Technische Universit\"at Dresden, Germany}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Published accuracy numbers for visual-inertial estimators are usually obtained by different
authors, with different parameter sets, initialisation shortcuts and evaluation scripts, and
they rarely report how often a system fails or where its computation time goes. We evaluate
four widely used stereo-inertial systems---ORB-SLAM3, OpenVINS, Kimera-VIO and NVIDIA
cuVSLAM---under one containerized protocol: identical CPU and memory limits, self-initialisation
from the sensors only (no ground-truth initialisation), the same \SE{} trajectory evaluation,
and per-module timing recorded inside each open-source system while it runs. On four sequences
(EuRoC \MHe{} and \VId{}, TUM-VI \corr{}, and an outdoor snow sequence recorded with an Intel
RealSense D435i), ORB-SLAM3 is the most accurate system on all indoor sequences
(0.025--0.045\,m APE RMSE), OpenVINS is the cheapest per frame (14--16\,ms mean) at
0.06\,m on EuRoC, and Kimera-VIO is accurate when it starts at rest but diverges on handheld
data without a stationary start. cuVSLAM in its standard raw-image mode does not reach
sub-metre accuracy on any sequence in our setup. All four failure cases are analysed and traced
to concrete causes: static IMU initialisation, camera distortion handling, frame loss at a
bit-depth conversion node, and a low-texture short-baseline scene. We also report a set of
configuration errors that silently corrupt results in multi-system benchmarks. The full
pipeline, configurations and raw logs are released.
\end{abstract}

visual-inertial odometry, SLAM, benchmarking, reproducibility, runtime profiling

%=============================================================================
\section{Introduction}
%=============================================================================

Visual-inertial odometry (VIO) and visual-inertial SLAM are the standard way to estimate the
6-DoF pose of drones, handheld devices and mobile robots where GNSS is unavailable or
unreliable. Several mature open-source systems exist, built on very different estimation
principles: filtering (OpenVINS~\cite{openvins}, based on the MSCKF~\cite{mourikis2007}),
fixed-lag smoothing on a factor graph (Kimera-VIO~\cite{kimeravio}), and keyframe-based bundle
adjustment with loop closure (ORB-SLAM3~\cite{orbslam3}). GPU-accelerated systems such as NVIDIA
cuVSLAM~\cite{cuvslam,korovko2025cuvslam} promise high frame rates on embedded hardware.

Choosing between these systems is hard because their published results are not directly
comparable. Papers report numbers produced with different parameter sets, sometimes with
ground-truth initialisation, with different trajectory alignment (\SE{} or \Sim{}), and on
different hardware. Failures are usually not reported at all, and runtime is given as a single
mean per frame, if at all.

This paper reports a benchmark in which all systems are run by the same script, in containers
with the same resource limits, initialise themselves from the sensor data only, and are
evaluated by the same evaluation code. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A containerized, scripted benchmark pipeline (one command per system and dataset) for
        ORB-SLAM3, OpenVINS, Kimera-VIO and cuVSLAM on ROS~2 bags and EuRoC-format folders.
  \item Accuracy results on four sequences of increasing difficulty, including an outdoor
        snow sequence, evaluated with \SE{} alignment and reported together with matched-pose
        counts and run-to-run spread.
  \item An analysis of every failed run, each traced to a concrete cause.
  \item Per-module runtime measured inside each open-source system with timestamped scoped
        timers, shown as Gantt charts of real frames and as per-module statistics. Every
        figure is generated from the logs written during the runs.
  \item A list of configuration pitfalls we found while building the benchmark; each of them
        changed results silently.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
%=============================================================================

\textbf{Visual-inertial estimators.} The MSCKF~\cite{mourikis2007} removes landmarks from the
filter state by null-space projection and keeps the filter cost linear in the number of
features; OpenVINS~\cite{openvins} is a modular implementation with first-estimates Jacobians
for consistency~\cite{huang2011fej}. Optimisation-based methods include
OKVIS~\cite{leutenegger2015okvis}, VINS-Mono~\cite{qin2018vinsmono} and
Kimera-VIO~\cite{kimeravio}, which combines IMU preintegration~\cite{forster2017} with
incremental smoothing by iSAM2~\cite{dellaert2012isam2}. ORB-SLAM3~\cite{orbslam3} extends
ORB-SLAM2~\cite{murartal2017orbslam2} with visual-inertial maximum-a-posteriori initialisation,
ORB features~\cite{rublee2011orb}, DBoW2 place recognition~\cite{galvez2012dbow2} and a
multi-map atlas. Direct methods such as DSO~\cite{engel2017dso} and
ROVIO~\cite{bloesch2017rovio} avoid feature descriptors. cuVSLAM~\cite{korovko2025cuvslam}
runs a KLT-based~\cite{lucaskanade1981,bouguet2001klt} stereo-inertial front end and its
optimisation on the GPU and is distributed in binary form as part of Isaac ROS~\cite{cuvslam}.

\textbf{Benchmarks.} EuRoC~\cite{euroc} and TUM-VI~\cite{schubert2018tumvi} are the standard
visual-inertial datasets with motion-capture ground truth. Delmerico and
Scaramuzza~\cite{delmerico2018benchmark} compared monocular VIO systems on EuRoC, including
runtime on embedded boards. Trajectory evaluation methodology is discussed by Zhang and
Scaramuzza~\cite{zhang2018tutorial} and Sturm et al.~\cite{sturm2012tum}; we use the evo
toolbox~\cite{grupp2017evo} with Umeyama alignment~\cite{umeyama1991}. Compared to earlier
comparisons, we (i) forbid ground-truth initialisation, (ii) report failures explicitly instead
of tuning them away, and (iii) measure per-module time inside the running systems instead of
reporting only a per-frame mean.

\textbf{ORB-SLAM3}~\cite{orbslam3} tracks ORB features in every frame against a local map, inserts
keyframes, optimises them by local bundle adjustment (BA) in a separate thread, and corrects drift
by loop closing in a third thread. It is the only system here that closes loops.

\textbf{OpenVINS}~\cite{openvins} tracks KLT features and updates an MSCKF filter with a sliding
window of clones. A small number of long-lived features are kept in the state as SLAM landmarks.
It runs in a single thread per frame and has no loop closure.

\textbf{Kimera-VIO}~\cite{kimeravio} tracks features with KLT in every frame, performs stereo
matching on keyframes, and optimises keyframe states with IMU preintegration factors and
structureless vision factors in an iSAM2 fixed-lag smoother. The mesher builds a 3D mesh from
the keyframe landmarks. Its loop-closure module is disabled here, so it is compared as odometry.

\textbf{cuVSLAM}~\cite{korovko2025cuvslam} is NVIDIA's CUDA implementation of stereo(-inertial)
odometry and mapping. We use the official Isaac ROS Visual SLAM package~\cite{cuvslam} without
modification, record its odometry output (\texttt{/visual\_slam/tracking/odometry}) and use its
standard raw-image mode, in which the node receives the distorted images together with the
distortion model and undistorts internally. The library is closed source.

ORB-SLAM3 with a KLT front end was part of our original plan but is excluded from this paper:
its source lacks the timing instrumentation used for the other systems, and its runs have not yet
been repeated under the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:method}.

\section{Benchmark Design}
\label{sec:method}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Pipeline and resource limits}

Each system runs in its own Docker image on one laptop (AMD Ryzen~7 4800H, 8 cores/16 threads,
22\,GB RAM, NVIDIA RTX~3050 Laptop GPU with 4\,GB). A single script,
\texttt{run\_benchmark.sh <system> <dataset>}, starts the container, feeds the data, collects the
trajectory and timing log, and a second script evaluates the run. Every container is limited to
2~CPU cores and 3\,GB of RAM, a budget typical of an embedded companion computer. The limits are
set with \texttt{docker run --cpus --memory} and are the same for every system (one cuVSLAM
repetition used 4 cores, Section~\ref{sec:cuvslam}).

\textbf{Input routes.} Systems that are ROS nodes (OpenVINS, cuVSLAM) receive the ROS~2 bag,
played at real-time rate. ORB-SLAM3 and Kimera-VIO provide offline example programs that read
EuRoC-format folders (\texttt{mav0/cam0}, \texttt{cam1}, \texttt{imu0}); for TUM-VI and
\sued{} these folders are produced from the same bags by a converter that keeps the original
image and IMU timestamps and values. The offline readers process every frame; a frame that takes
longer than the camera period delays the next one instead of being dropped. ROS nodes can drop
frames if they fall behind, which we count from gaps in the output.

\textbf{Bit depth.} TUM-VI images are 16-bit. None of the systems processes 16-bit images
(cuVSLAM's ROS node accepts only \texttt{mono8} and \texttt{rgb8}), so all receive the 8 most
significant bits. We verified that the conversion inside OpenVINS (\texttt{cv\_bridge}) and our
offline conversion (\texttt{>{}>~8}) produce identical pixels.

\textbf{Initialisation.} All systems initialise from sensor data only. In particular,
Kimera-VIO's EuRoC example parameters initialise the state from ground truth; we use its
IMU-based initialisation instead (\texttt{autoInitialize: 1}). This initialisation assumes the
platform is at rest, so each Kimera run starts at the first frame of a stationary window
(frame 460 on \MHe{}, 20 on \VId{}, 70 on \corr{}, the calmest window of that handheld
sequence).

\textbf{EuRoC}~\cite{euroc}. \MHe{} is a slow flight in a machine hall with a long stationary
start; \VId{} is the most aggressive of the Vicon-room flights, with fast rotations and motion
blur. Both use the official calibration.

\textbf{TUM-VI \corr{}}~\cite{schubert2018tumvi}. A handheld walk through corridors with
fisheye stereo cameras. The motion-capture ground truth is only available in the room at the
start and end, i.e.\ for 52 of the 300\,s. An error on this sequence therefore mostly measures
the drift accumulated between leaving and re-entering the room.

\textbf{\sued{}.} An outdoor sequence over a snow-covered field, recorded as a ROS~2 bag with
an Intel RealSense D435i (infrared stereo pair at 848$\times$480 and 30\,Hz, IMU at 200\,Hz) and
a PX4 GNSS receiver. The bag contains no camera calibration. We use the rectified infrared
streams with zero distortion, the D435i baseline $b = 49.96$\,mm and IMU extrinsics of the
D435i configuration shipped with ORB-SLAM3, and measured the focal length from the bag itself:
the camera computes its depth image with the factory calibration, so
$\text{depth} \times \text{disparity} = f_x b$. Stereo matching on ten frames spread over the
sequence gives $f_x b = 21.05$\,px\,m and thus $f_x = 421.35$\,px, consistent with the
640$\times$480 factory value scaled to this resolution. The principal point is set to the image
centre. The reference trajectory consists of the 1173 3D GNSS fixes (mean reported horizontal
accuracy 0.53\,m), converted to a local ENU frame; there is no orientation reference.

\subsection{Metrics}

\textbf{Accuracy.} Estimated and reference trajectories are associated by nearest timestamp
within 0.05\,s and aligned by an \SE{} Umeyama fit~\cite{umeyama1991}, without scale correction,
since all systems use stereo. We report the RMSE of the absolute translation error (APE) as
computed by evo~\cite{grupp2017evo} and give the number of matched poses, because the systems
output poses at different rates (Kimera-VIO writes keyframe poses only). \Sim{} alignment is used
only as a diagnostic for scale errors.

\textbf{Runtime.} For the three open-source systems we record, for every frame and every
module, the start and end timestamps of the module's execution and its thread. OpenVINS has
built-in scoped timers. For ORB-SLAM3 we placed scoped timers at the existing
\texttt{REGISTER\_TIMES} measurement points (released as a patch). For Kimera-VIO we time each
pipeline module's processing call. From these logs we draw Gantt charts of consecutive real
frames and compute per-module statistics. Module boundaries follow each system's own code
structure, so the per-frame front-end time is the most comparable quantity across systems.
cuVSLAM is closed source; its internal modules cannot be timed, and we do not show a module
profile for it.

\textbf{EuRoC.} ORB-SLAM3 is the most accurate system on both EuRoC sequences
(0.045\,m and 0.025\,m), followed by OpenVINS (0.065\,m and 0.062\,m) and Kimera-VIO

its fast motion excites the IMU well and gives the front ends more parallax, while \MHe{} has
long, slow segments. ORB-SLAM3 kept a single map throughout \VId{} without any loop
correction, so its accuracy there comes from tracking and local BA alone. OpenVINS needs the
platform to move before its initialiser starts and outputs no pose for the first $\approx$45\,s
of \MHe{} (the stationary part of the sequence); Kimera-VIO starts in the same stationary
window by design. Fig.~\ref{fig:apetime} shows the error over time: Kimera-VIO's error is
largest directly after initialisation on \MHe{} (0.47\,m) and decreases once the platform
moves, which points to an imperfect initial gravity and bias estimate rather than to front-end
problems.

\textbf{TUM-VI \corr{}.} The mocap ground truth covers only the start and end room, so the
\SE{} fit aligns the two room segments jointly and the APE measures mainly the drift accumulated
in between. Evaluated on the start segment alone, OpenVINS has 0.019\,m and ORB-SLAM3 0.011\,m
error; at the end of the 299\,m walk, OpenVINS has drifted by 1.40\,m (0.47\,\% of the path),
ORB-SLAM3 by 0.065\,m. ORB-SLAM3 closes the loop twice when it re-enters the room (two loop
corrections of $\approx$930\,ms each in its timing log), so its 0.034\,m is a SLAM result and
OpenVINS' 0.53\,m an odometry result; the two should not be compared as the same quantity.

\subsection{Failure analysis}
\label{sec:failures}

\textbf{Kimera-VIO on \corr{}: divergence from static initialisation.} The estimate runs away
within seconds and reaches $\approx$200\,m/s after 17\,s. The front end is not the cause:
$\approx$90\,\% of frames are tracked as valid stereo frames. The cause is the IMU
initialisation, which assumes a stationary platform. \corr{} is handheld and never at rest, so
the rotation during the initialisation window is absorbed into the gyroscope bias, estimated as
$-0.232$\,rad/s on one axis, one to two orders of magnitude above a realistic bias. With the
default bias prior (\texttt{imu\_bias\_init\_sigma}~$=10^{-3}$) the wrong bias is never
corrected, the gravity direction tilts, and part of gravity is integrated as acceleration. On
\VId{}, where the same parameters start in a 3\,s stationary window, the initial bias estimate is
realistic ($<$0.08\,rad/s) and the run is accurate. We report this as a failure of the
static-initialisation assumption and did not tune the bias prior per sequence.

\textbf{cuVSLAM on EuRoC: distortion handling in raw-image mode.}
\label{sec:cuvslam}
On \MHe{} the shape of the trajectory is right but every motion is enlarged: \Sim{} alignment
finds a scale of 0.77, i.e.\ the trajectory is $\approx$30\,\% too large, and the error is
1.55\,m even after scale correction. Two runs with identical configuration gave 1.31\,m and
1.97\,m. We checked the camera-to-IMU transforms, intrinsics, distortion coefficients and their
order, and the IMU frame and noise parameters. In a separate experiment outside the benchmark
protocol, the same images rectified beforehand with OpenCV reduced the error to 0.063\,m, so the
error arises from the node's handling of EuRoC's strong radial-tangential distortion
($k_1 \approx -0.28$) in raw-image mode, not from the calibration. On \VId{} the problem is
amplified by fast motion: the estimate stops following the true motion (2.65\,m; the \Sim{} fit
shrinks the trajectory to a scale of 0.20, which is not a meaningful scale estimate). Few frames
were lost on EuRoC (58--64 gaps longer than one frame period).

\textbf{cuVSLAM on \corr{}: frame loss.} cuVSLAM produced poses for only 3873--3971 of 5990
stereo frames, with $\approx$1600 gaps of 100--350\,ms. The 16-bit images must be converted to
8-bit by an extra ROS node between bag and cuVSLAM, and frames are lost on this path; which
frames are lost changes from run to run, and three runs gave 1.54, 4.15 and 2.56\,m APE. Doubling
the CPU limit to 4 cores did not reduce the gaps, so the loss is not simply a CPU shortage of the
conversion node. During these runs we also found and corrected an inverted camera-to-IMU
transform in the launch file (Section~\ref{sec:pitfalls}); the frame loss dominated the result
both before and after the correction. We therefore report the range and consider the
\corr{} result not reproducible in this configuration.

\textbf{ORB-SLAM3 on \sued{}: low texture and short baseline.} ORB-SLAM3 waits for
$\approx$26\,s until the platform accelerates, then initialises the map and IMU successfully,
but loses tracking repeatedly (615 ``fail to track local map'' events, 13 map resets; each map
survives about 5\,s). Its largest map covers 22.8\,s, over which it estimates $\approx$10\,m/s
while GNSS shows $\approx$3.5\,m/s; the drift is IMU dead reckoning after visual tracking was
lost, and the resulting 24.5\,m APE is not an accuracy result. The scene explains the failure:
the lower half of the image is uniform snow, most features lie on a tree line tens of metres
away, and with a 50\,mm baseline and $f_x = 421$\,px a point at 20\,m has a disparity of
$\approx$1\,px. Very few points are close enough to constrain translation, while the platform
moves $\approx$12\,cm between frames. Nominal principal point and IMU extrinsics may add to this,
but the system initialises with them, and the measured $f_x b$ matches the camera. The other
systems have not yet been run on this sequence.

\textbf{OpenVINS} is the cheapest system per frame: a complete filter step takes
14--16\,ms on average and stays below the 50\,ms camera period even at the 95th percentile on
all sequences. Feature tracking and the SLAM-landmark update are the largest parts; the MSCKF
update itself costs 1--2\,ms. All work happens in one thread per frame (Fig.~\ref{fig:gantt}).

\textbf{ORB-SLAM3} spends 34--43\,ms per frame in its tracking thread, of which ORB extraction is
the largest single part (up to 47\,\% on \MHe{}). The mean is below the camera period, but the
95th percentile (69--83\,ms) is not: some frames take longer than 50\,ms, which the offline
reader absorbs by delaying the next frame. Local BA takes 170--220\,ms per keyframe and runs
concurrently in the local-mapping thread (Fig.~\ref{fig:gantt}), so its cost does not add to
per-frame latency as long as the second core is free. The two loop corrections on \corr{} took
$\approx$930\,ms each in the loop-closing thread without blocking tracking.

\textbf{Kimera-VIO}'s front-end time is strongly bimodal: the median is 3.7--3.9\,ms, but frames
that become keyframes carry feature detection and stereo matching, which raises the mean to
33\,ms and the 95th percentile to $\approx$127\,ms. The back end (iSAM2 update) takes
51--90\,ms per keyframe. The front-end and back-end run in separate threads.

\textbf{cuVSLAM} exposes no internal timing. What can be measured from outside---output rate and
gaps---is reported in Section~\ref{sec:failures}. A module profile would have to be invented,
so we do not show one.

%=============================================================================
\section{Discussion}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Configuration pitfalls}
\label{sec:pitfalls}

Several errors we found while building the benchmark changed results without causing any
error message. We list them because they are easy to repeat in any multi-system comparison.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Inverted extrinsics.} TUM-VI publishes $T_{\text{cam,imu}}$, while a ROS
        transform from the body (IMU) frame to the camera expects $T_{\text{imu,cam}}$. The
        cuVSLAM \corr{} launch file used the former; the result was a plausible trajectory with
        steady vertical drift.
  \item \textbf{Wrong sensor model.} The first \sued{} configuration was a public D455 colour
        configuration (95\,mm baseline, lens distortion, 400\,Hz IMU) for data recorded with a
        D435i (50\,mm, rectified infrared streams, 200\,Hz). It ran without errors.
  \item \textbf{Ground-truth initialisation.} Kimera-VIO's EuRoC example parameters initialise
        from ground truth; published-style accuracy is obtained only with this shortcut.
  \item \textbf{Ground-truth construction.} The original \sued{} reference repeated the last
        GNSS fix at every camera timestamp and was zero before the first fix; we now use the
        fixes themselves at their own timestamps.
  \item \textbf{Silent defaults.} The runner script mapped unknown dataset names to \MHe{},
        so a run on another sequence silently re-evaluated \MHe{}.
  \item \textbf{Stale outputs.} A run that produced no poses left the previous run's plots in
        place; result folders must be cleared before each run.
  \item \textbf{Bit depth.} 16-bit TUM-VI images must be reduced to 8 bits for every system;
        doing it identically for all keeps the comparison fair, but an extra conversion node in
        the live data path can lose frames (cuVSLAM, \corr{}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Choosing a system}

For accuracy on indoor data with a reasonable start, ORB-SLAM3 is the best of the tested systems
and, with loop closure, the only one that removes drift when revisiting places. It also has the
highest per-frame cost and per-frame latency spikes above the camera period on two cores.
OpenVINS is 2--3$\times$ cheaper per frame, never exceeded the camera period at the 95th
percentile, and was the most robust system in our tests (no failure on any indoor sequence), at
roughly twice ORB-SLAM3's error on EuRoC and with unbounded drift on long trajectories.
Kimera-VIO is competitive when it can initialise at rest, which suits robots that start
standing still, but not handheld devices. cuVSLAM's raw-image mode should not be used with
strongly distorted lenses without first checking the scale of the output; rectifying the images
before the node is a promising but separate configuration that we will evaluate under the full
protocol.

\subsection{Threats to validity}

Each configuration was run once, except where a spread is given; run-to-run variation of the
multi-threaded and GPU systems is therefore only partially quantified. Instrumentation overhead
was not measured separately; the timers record two timestamps per module call. CPU and GPU
utilisation were not recorded. The \sued{} calibration is partly nominal and its reference is
GNSS without orientation. The \corr{} reference covers 17\,\% of the sequence. Default
parameters shipped for EuRoC and TUM-VI were used for each system; per-sequence tuning could
change individual results, but it would also undo the purpose of a common protocol.

%=============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%=============================================================================

Under one containerized protocol with self-initialisation and identical evaluation,
ORB-SLAM3 was the most accurate system on all indoor sequences (0.025--0.045\,m APE), OpenVINS
the cheapest (14--16\,ms per frame) and most robust, and Kimera-VIO accurate only with a
stationary start. cuVSLAM in raw-image mode did not reach sub-metre accuracy in our setup, for
reasons we traced to distortion handling and frame loss. No system tracked the low-texture
outdoor snow sequence with a 50\,mm baseline. Measured per-module timelines show where each
system spends its time and which costs are hidden in background threads.

Future work: running OpenVINS, Kimera-VIO and cuVSLAM on the outdoor sequence; an offline
8-bit conversion and a pre-rectified input for cuVSLAM; repeated runs of every configuration;
CPU/GPU utilisation and end-to-end latency; and the dual-rate KLT variant of ORB-SLAM3 under
the same protocol.

\section*{Reproducibility}
Docker set-up, run and evaluation scripts, configuration files, the ORB-SLAM3 and Kimera-VIO
timing patches, and all raw trajectories and timing logs are in the project repository. All
figures and tables are generated by \texttt{scripts/plot\_paper\_figures.py} and
\texttt{scripts/plot\_gantt.py} from these logs; \texttt{results/summary.csv} lists every
number in Table~\ref{tab:ape}.
